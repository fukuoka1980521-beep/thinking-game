# NEW LIFE — Owner Review: Six People and Three Events (V1)

This is written for you, not for a developer. No jargon unless it's the plain word for the thing.
The question at the end is yours alone: **do you want to spend 30 days with these people?**

---

## 1. The game, in one page

NEW LIFE is a small text game about moving into a new town for a month and getting to know six
people who live there. Nothing about the town waits for you. People have their own plans, their own
friendships, their own problems, and their own bad habits — they move forward whether you pay
attention or not. You can help, stay out of it, say the wrong thing, or genuinely change how
something turns out — but you are one person in their town, not the reason their lives happen.

This redesign starts from a different place than the last one did. The old version built a
30-day calendar first and then tried to fit six people into it. The Owner's own playtest showed the
result: the six people felt too similar, conversation didn't create real interest, and there wasn't
much pull to find out what happens next. This time, the six people were built first — real,
different personalities, real needs, real ways they get in their own way — and everything else (the
events, the eventual 30-day story) will be derived from them, not the other way around.

**Nothing has been written for Day 1 through Day 30 yet.** This document, and the technical work
behind it, is the "who are these people, and what happens when their lives collide" layer. If you
say yes to these six people, the next phase turns this into an actual month you can play.

---

## 2. The six people

For each: who they are, the honest human pattern behind them, their personality, what they're
stuck on, how they talk, and — importantly — both how they might change this month, and what it
looks like if they don't (both are meant to be real, complete outcomes, not "pass/fail").

### Hina, 26 — opening a small bakery

A talented, precise baker, confident and detailed the moment you talk about the food itself — and
noticeably quieter and more careful the moment the conversation drifts toward whether the business
will actually work. Her last shop, elsewhere, closed over money trouble she never told anyone about
until it was too late to fix. She's decided, without quite admitting it to herself, that needing
help means the same thing is about to happen again. **Real-human core**: your own experience of
becoming defensive when real users criticized something you'd built, and only later realizing a
creator has to become their own harshest critic before outside criticism can actually help — plus a
general, common pattern of treating "asking for help" as an admission of failure rather than an
ordinary act of trust.
**How she talks**: sharp, technical, and fast about baking; short and hedged and self-interrupting
about anything personal. Dry, rare humor. Over-apologizes for small things, under-apologizes for
real ones.
**Confirmed working well already**: you played a scene with her directly and reported it felt
completely natural — direct, warm without being instantly close, opinionated, able to disagree
gently, small and believable rather than dramatic. That specific quality of conversation is now the
bar the other five are measured against (see Section 8 of this document).
**How she might change**: by actually asking someone for real help before the outcome is already
decided, instead of managing everything alone the way she did last time.
**How she might not**: she can still open (or not open) her shop this month — that part isn't up to
whether she opens up emotionally — but she does it entirely on her own, tells no one the real reason
if it gets hard, and ends the month exactly as guarded as she started. That's a legitimate, dignified
outcome. Not everyone opens up in a month, and the game should be honest about that.

### Daisuke, 45 — runs the barbershop

The easiest person in town to talk to. He draws everyone else out mid-haircut, genuinely enjoys
people's stories — and goes warm, funny, and completely evasive the instant a question points back
at him. He's been sitting on two small decisions for a long time: whether to finally renovate his
shop, and whether to answer an old friend's unanswered New Year's card. Neither is urgent. Neither
has moved in months, or a year.
**Real-human core**: your own pattern of repeatedly pushing the real, uncomfortable decision down
the road while staying busy with things that feel like progress — and the real cost that shows up
later, once the decision can't be avoided anymore.
**How he talks**: warm, easy, conversational, full of questions about other people, almost none
about himself. Redirects with a joke, every time, smoothly enough that most people don't notice.
**How he might change**: actually deciding one of the two things — not necessarily the "brave" one,
just an actual decision, made on purpose.
**How he might not**: both things resolve on their own eventual schedule regardless (the world
doesn't wait for him either) — he can go the whole month exactly as warm, exactly as evasive, and
exactly as stuck, and that's a real, complete version of him too.

### Jin, 57 — the town's handyman

Quietly excellent at everything he does, always around, never asks for anything. He shows he cares
entirely by doing — fixing something no one asked him to fix — and would never directly say he'd
like steadier work, or that he'd occasionally like to be asked how HE is, rather than just thanked
for the work.
**Real-human core**: two of your own patterns combined — competence built from real experience that
occasionally skips double-checking the specific, current situation; and the observation that pure
independence eventually plateaus without one well-timed, specific gesture from someone else.
**How he talks**: almost nothing. Short, plain, dry, deadpan. Comfortable with silence in a way
almost no one else in town is.
**How he might change**: saying, once, plainly, what he actually wants — to someone he trusts.
**How he might not**: he stays exactly himself — competent, quietly present, entirely unresentful.
This has to read as a whole, real person choosing not to change, not a sad "failure."

### Yohei, 63 — runs the general store

Gruff, blunt, business-minded, respected. Generous with strangers in small practical ways; flatly
unable to reach out to his own adult son, who he hasn't spoken to in over a year, for a reason
smaller than most of the problems he solves for other people without a second thought. He reads his
own guardedness as "being careful," not as anything related to his son at all.
**Real-human core**: your own experience of a practical shortcut taken under pressure quietly
damaging the very thing it was meant to protect.
**How he talks**: short, blunt, numbers-first. Never says the real thing directly. Apologizes
through action (an extra item slipped into a bag), never in words.
**How he might change**: accepting that his guardedness is fear, not prudence, and doing one small,
real thing differently — not fixing the relationship, just making the first small contact.
**How he might not**: the son thread resolves on its own regardless (a real family, moving on its
own timeline) — but Yohei's own self-image never gets examined, and he ends the month exactly as
gruff, successful, and privately unchanged as he started.

### Miyoko, 68 — runs the café

Endlessly warm and patient with absolutely everyone, on purpose, so that no one ever feels like a
favorite. The cost of that: it's genuinely hard to tell when SHE needs something, since her warmth
never looks different from a request. Her grown daughter wants her to close the café and move
closer; Miyoko is certain she already knows what her daughter wants and needs — she just hasn't
actually asked in a while.
**How she talks**: soft, warm, a little rambling, asks constant questions about you specifically to
avoid being asked about herself.
**How she might change**: actually stating a real preference of her own, once, out loud — about her
daughter, or simply about her own life, instead of continuing to guess.
**How she might not**: she keeps deferring, keeps everyone equally comfortable, and the assumption
about her daughter stays untested. A common, ordinary, non-tragic way for a relationship to continue.

### Fumiko, 70 — retired teacher, runs the community Hall

Brisk, direct, capable, occasionally misread as short-tempered (she doesn't notice the gap). Since
retiring, she's quietly measuring her own usefulness against the Hall — and refuses even small,
sensible changes there (like finally replacing an old, wobbly bench) because letting go of any of it
feels like admitting she's no longer essential.
**How she talks**: short, precise, sometimes formal. States and directs rather than asks. Rare,
stiff apologies.
**How she might change**: actually handing a real, ongoing piece of responsibility to someone else
(most naturally Jin) — proving to herself she can still matter without personally controlling every
detail.
**How she might not**: she keeps running the Hall entirely herself, exactly as capably as always,
and stays just as controlling and just as quietly under-connected as she started.

## 3. How they relate to each other (not everyone is close to everyone)

- Hina and Daisuke genuinely like each other. Jin and Miyoko genuinely like each other. Miyoko and
  Fumiko are childhood friends — her deepest bond in town. Yohei and Daisuke are the town's oldest,
  warmest friendship.
- Two real, honest misunderstandings sit in the cast, and they mirror each other: Hina reads Yohei's
  everyday gruffness as personal dislike, and Yohei has no idea it lands that way — he thinks he's
  just being careful with a newcomer, the way he is with everyone. Separately, Jin and Fumiko each
  privately think the other sees their relationship as purely transactional (just paid work), and
  neither has ever actually tested that assumption. Neither misunderstanding is anyone's fault; both
  are the kind of thing that happens between real, decent people.
- Fumiko gives Daisuke more advice than she ever asks for herself — even her closest give-and-take
  friendship runs a little one-directional, which fits her.
- All of this moves on its own, in the background, with or without you around — the same discipline
  the earlier version of this game already committed to, now given to relationships between the six
  people, not just to "the world."

## 4. Three things that happen to the whole town this month

Instead of six separate personal problems that each person deals with alone, the plan is three
shared events that hit everyone differently — because that's what actually reveals who someone is:
watching how six different people respond to the exact same real pressure.

**Event 1 — The Preview Stall.** The town lets Hina set up a small test stall inside the community
Hall on an ordinary market day — not the big festival, just a real, smaller rehearsal. But it's not
just Hina's project: Fumiko has to actually hand over the space, Yohei handles the stock, Jin builds
the physical stall, Miyoko provides the coffee, Daisuke offers his shopfront as overflow. Everyone
has something real at stake, in their own way, even in something this small.

**Event 2 — The Wiring Problem.** During the stall, a piece of secondhand equipment (which Yohei
sourced to save money, wired in a hurry by Jin, on a setup Fumiko hadn't actually double-checked)
sparks and briefly catches. No one is seriously hurt — Jin gets a minor burn catching it fast — but
the stall shuts early, and word spreads that "something went wrong at the Hall." Because more than
one person's choices genuinely contributed, there's no single person to blame, and each of the six
reacts to that in a completely different way (see below). This isn't a manufactured disaster — it's
the believable, mundane kind of thing that actually happens when six well-meaning people share a
small project.

**Event 3 — The Market Day Decision.** In the days after, the town has to decide: does this become a
regular thing? And if so, who's actually responsible for what — the space, the safety, the stock,
the setup? This is where everything from Events 1 and 2 gets spent: does Fumiko finally hand real
responsibility to someone else? Does Yohei own his part in the equipment choice, or let it stay
vague? Does Jin, for once, say what he needs? Does Hina ask for real help this time, or try alone
again? Does Daisuke actually commit to something ongoing? Does Miyoko say what she wants, for once?

There is no single "correct" ending to this decision. Depending on how the month actually went, the
market day could become a real, regular fixture; or happen once more, informally, with nothing
really resolved; or simply not happen again — and none of those outcomes are written as a win or a
loss.

## 5. How each of the six reacts to Event 2 — the moment that shows them clearest

This is the best single illustration of why they're different people, not six versions of the same
person:

- **Hina** spirals inward — reads it as proof her business is cursed the same way the last one was,
  and quietly starts pulling back before anyone tells her to.
- **Daisuke** defuses the tension with a joke almost immediately — genuinely helps the mood, and
  also means no one actually talks through what happened for at least a day.
- **Jin** gets hurt catching the spark, downplays it instantly, and privately carries real guilt —
  he'd half-suspected the old equipment beforehand and said nothing.
- **Yohei** minimizes his own part — "the equipment was fine when I checked it" — not quite lying,
  just not quite looking at it honestly yet.
- **Miyoko** over-apologizes and gives away a week of free coffee, despite having zero actual
  responsibility for what happened.
- **Fumiko** takes it hardest as proof she's less capable than she used to be, and overcorrects by
  unilaterally announcing new rules instead of talking to anyone first.

Six real, different people, meeting the same afternoon.

## 6. Example interactions

**Asking Jin for help, blind, without checking on him first**: "Fine." A brief pause. Already moving
to the next task. Nothing more offered unless asked specifically.

**Asking Jin directly what he actually needs, once real trust exists**: a short, plain sentence — no
speech, just one real thing, said once. He won't say it twice if it isn't received well.

**Confronting Fumiko early and bluntly about a mistake**: she doesn't fold — she gets MORE
controlling in the short term, not less. She responds far better to a plain, calm question than to
pressure.

**Asking Miyoko what SHE wants, not what she can offer you**: a genuine pause — "goodness, no one's
really asked me that in a while" — before she actually answers, because it doesn't happen to her
often.

**A conversation with Hina about her actual product** (the one already tested and confirmed working
well by you directly): direct answer, a concrete number, a small piece of her own thinking behind
it, a light disagreement when it's warranted, and a natural next thing to say — never a dead end,
never a script reciting shop hours.

## 7. Five different playthroughs, same six people, same three events

We ran five imagined playthroughs with different kinds of players to check that the same cast
produces genuinely different stories, not one story with cosmetic variation:

1. **A very social player**, who talks to everyone but goes deep with no one: mostly builds broad,
   shallow goodwill. Only Daisuke actually moves this month — he responds well to steady, easy
   company even without being pushed.
2. **A practical, helpful, privacy-respecting player**: catches a real technical problem early by
   asking a plain, factual question — and later gets Yohei, uniquely, to honestly own his part in
   the equipment mistake, because Yohei responds to plain fact far better than to feelings.
3. **A blunt, confrontational player**: backfires badly with Hina (she reads directness as exactly
   the judgment she fears) and with Fumiko (who digs in when challenged) — but works surprisingly
   well with Jin, who actually prefers a direct question to a soft one, and ends up speaking up for
   himself for the first time this month.
4. **A passive player who mostly watches**: nobody privately changes this month — and importantly,
   this still produces a complete, coherent story. The town keeps moving, the friendships keep
   going, the market-day decision still gets made by the six of them, on their own.
5. **An inconsistent player** — kind in some moments, careless in others (forgets a promise to
   Miyoko, laughs at the wrong moment with Jin): the damage isn't uniform. Yohei, who doesn't expect
   much from newcomers anyway, barely notices. Miyoko and Jin, who both track reliability closely in
   their own quiet ways, become noticeably cooler toward the player specifically — without ever
   saying so out loud, which is exactly how each of them would actually handle it.

The same player behavior helped one person and hurt another in more than one of these runs — proof
there's no single "right" way to play that makes everyone like you.

## 8. What's real, what's composite, and what's invented

- **Directly based on your own real experience** (four of the six main characters use at least one
  of these): Hina's defensiveness about criticism and reluctance to ask for help; Daisuke's pattern
  of postponing a real decision while staying busy; Jin's competence-built-on-experience occasionally
  skipping a fresh check, plus the idea that independence plateaus without one well-timed gesture
  from someone else; Yohei's practical shortcut under pressure quietly undermining what it protected.
- **Built from established psychological research** (used only as a design framework — never shown
  to you or the player as a score or a diagnosis): broad personality tendencies (Big Five), how
  assertive/warm each person tends to be with others (the interpersonal circumplex), what each
  person needs to feel free, capable, and connected (self-determination theory), and the idea that
  the SAME person can act very differently in different situations without that being inconsistent
  (person-by-situation psychology) — full citations are recorded in the technical files.
- **Real consultation records were not used, deliberately.** We did not verify permission to reuse
  any specific real client/consultation story for this purpose, and did not go looking for one this
  round — that boundary was treated as more important than filling a category. Where a pattern
  needed a shape research and your own experience didn't already cover (Miyoko's and Fumiko's
  characters specifically), it's honestly labeled as invented, not claimed as anyone's real story.
- **Fully invented, for story connective tissue**: the specific plot mechanics of the three events
  themselves (the stall, the equipment, the town decision) — grounded in the human patterns above,
  but not claimed as anyone's literal real experience.

## One honest gap, found by testing, not hidden

We ran a blind test: a fresh, uninformed reader was given 60 short lines from the six people, with
their names removed, and asked to match each line back to a voice description. Four of the six were
told apart clearly and correctly, most of the time (Jin, Hina, Miyoko, Yohei). **Daisuke and Fumiko
were confused with each other more than a third of the time** — specifically on short, plain,
factual answers (like "what do you do here"), where Daisuke's usual warmth doesn't show up because
the question doesn't invite it, and he ends up sounding as clipped as Fumiko by default. Every line,
individually, still read as natural, specific, human dialogue, not generic or robotic — this isn't a
naturalness problem, it's a "these two specific people sound too alike in this one kind of moment"
problem. It's a small, precisely locatable thing to fix (giving Daisuke's short answers a bit more
of his own warmth), not evidence the whole approach doesn't work — but it's real, it was found by
actually testing rather than assuming, and it's reported here rather than smoothed over.

## The question

Do you want to spend 30 days with these six people?

If yes — the next phase builds the actual day-by-day month from this foundation, the same way the
prior version was built, but this time starting from these people and these three events instead of
starting from a calendar.

If not — tell us what's actually wrong with one of them specifically, and that becomes the next
round's real, scoped target, the same disciplined way every prior repair in this project has worked.
