## Premise

A 57-year-old man, no name given in dialogue (the game never needs one — he is "you"), left the
job he'd held through several roles — factory floor, sales, management, personnel, accounting,
business administration, in that order, over three decades — with no next job lined up. His wife
died within the past two years; this is never explained on screen and never confessed outright,
but it is why there was nothing left tying him to where he used to live. He applied, on his own,
for a 30-day trial residency in Challenge Town (チャレンジ町), a small regional city that has
built a quiet policy of not getting in the way of people trying to start over. He has 30 days in
a temporary room. On day 30 he returns the key. What happens after that is the entire game.

This is not a game about choosing a job from a list. It is a game about what a person becomes
after living somewhere for 30 days, and the job is just the visible tip of that.

## Protagonist

**Age/build**: 57, ordinary build, not stylized. Spent three decades in increasingly
office-bound, people-managing roles after starting on a factory floor — his hands remember
manual work slightly, his instincts don't.

**Money**: Severance plus modest savings. Not desperate, not comfortable. Enough for 30 days and
a few months after, not enough to drift indefinitely without it starting to matter.

**Family**: A grown daughter, living in another city, married, one child. They talk by phone
every week or two — she checks in, he keeps it short. She does not appear in Day 1-7. She is a
reachable, ordinary tether the game can call on later (a phone call is a legitimate scene, not an
extra character).

**The flaw, specific and load-bearing**: he evaluates. Three decades in personnel and business
administration trained him to read a room, an operation, a person's competence, without being
asked to. He is not smug about it and would deny doing it — but when Yohei explains how he orders
stock, some part of him is quietly reorganizing Yohei's supply chain in his head. When Miyoko
tells a story slightly out of order, he notices. He is not going to volunteer this — his other
habit, formed over the same thirty years, is knowing when unsolicited assessment is unwelcome —
but the tension between "I can see how this could run better" and "it is not my place, I am a
guest here, I have known these people four days" is a real, recurring friction, both internal and
occasionally external (Section: Conflict, below).

**Grief, specific**: he does not talk about his wife. Not stoically, not as a performance — he
genuinely does not bring her up, and mildly deflects if someone else does (changes the subject
within one or two lines, not dramatically). What surfaces instead: he notices small domestic
things other people wouldn't — the specific way Miyoko refills a sugar jar, a couple bickering
lightly in the street — and the noticing is never narrated as sadness. It's just there, a beat,
sometimes cut short by his own next line. The game never states what this means. By Day 15 it
should be legible to a player paying attention without ever being said.

**How he first behaves with each NPC**: Polite, competent-sounding, slightly over-formal — the
register of someone used to being the person other people report to, not the reverse. He asks
practical questions before personal ones. He offers help early (Section: Yohei) partly from
genuine impulse to be useful, partly because being useful is the only register he's fluent in
when he doesn't know a room yet.

**What changes over 30 days (arc, not plot)**: from a man managing his own transition like a
project — assessing options, weighing viability — toward a man who has actually lived somewhere
long enough that a choice stops being a spreadsheet and starts being "the place my life is,
now, whether or not I planned it." The arc is not "he learns to stop evaluating people" (that
would flatten him into a lesson) — it's that the evaluating stops being the only thing he's doing
in a room. He starts being late to conclusions because he's just talking.

## Kamiya (神谷) — Challenge Center

**Not a nav-role. His own stakes, stated plainly here (never narrated in-game):** the town funds
the Challenge Center's residency program annually, tied loosely to outcomes — how many of the
year's trial residents end up staying, working, or starting something in town. It is not phrased
to him as a hard quota, and he would resent hearing it called one, but he knows the number
matters when the budget committee meets in the spring, and he knows last year's number was soft.
He is not a cynical bureaucrat pushing placements to save his program — he genuinely believes in
the work — but the pressure is real, private, and occasionally shows.

**Age/work/daily life**: 36. Has worked at the Challenge Center for seven years, the last three
as the person residents actually deal with day to day (his supervisor, 所長 handles budget and
the town council, rarely appears). A typical day: morning intake or check-in meetings, midday
calls to local businesses about openings or apprenticeship slots, afternoon paperwork, occasional
site visits. He eats lunch alone at his desk more often than not.

**Money**: Ordinary town-government salary, not much. Rents a small apartment near the station.

**Family**: Grew up in a bigger city two prefectures over; came to Challenge Town for this job
specifically, knowing almost no one, the same year the residency program started. He is, in a
real sense, a seven-years-ago version of the protagonist, though he would not frame it that way
and does not volunteer it. Single. Was seeing someone for about two years until last spring; it
ended over his hours, not dramatically — she works in the prefectural capital and eventually
stopped visiting. He does not bring this up either, for reasons that rhyme with the protagonist's
own silences without either of them noticing the rhyme.

**Currently struggling with**: the budget pressure (above), and a specific unresolved case from
two years ago — a resident he placed with a small manufacturer who quit after five weeks and left
town without much explanation. Kamiya still doesn't fully know what went wrong, and it sits with
him as a data point he can't stop weighing against every new resident, including the protagonist.

**How he first treats the protagonist**: professional, a little rehearsed (he's done this intake
conversation dozens of times), reading him fast — the tone, the vocabulary, the way he answers
"what do you want to do" with a non-answer that's actually a management answer. Kamiya
pattern-matches him early, privately, to the type he thinks of as "the ones who talk about
options and never pick one" — not out of malice, out of pattern-fatigue. This reading is wrong,
or at least incomplete, and the friction of Kamiya being wrong about him (and slow to notice he's
wrong) is one of the book's real conflicts (see Conflict).

**What he does when the protagonist isn't around**: keeps running the Center. Meets with other
residents (never named on screen, just "another case" — the game should let this be felt, not
listed). Occasionally handles Center business that has nothing to do with the protagonist —
a shop closing, a training slot that fell through, a call with the prefecture. Goes home, doesn't
always cook, sometimes just gets something from the convenience store.

**Relationship to other NPCs**: knows Yohei's and Miyoko's and Jin's situations at a professional
remove — has placed people at Yohei's store before, has referred odd jobs to Jin, has the kind of
familiarity with the shopping street that comes from doing this job for seven years, without
being personally close to any of them the way old-town residents are close to each other. This
outsider-among-locals position is part of what lets him read the protagonist accurately later —
he's been the newcomer too.

**Contradiction, explicit**: preaches patience ("30日は焦る話じゃない") while privately running
a clock. Experienced enough to be genuinely good at this work; biased enough, from exactly that
experience, to sometimes see a pattern instead of a person.

**30-day change**: starts the month reading the protagonist as a type. Around the midpoint, gets
proven wrong in a small, specific way (not a speech — an action the protagonist takes that
doesn't fit the pattern) and has to recalibrate, visibly uncomfortable about being wrong. By
Day 30, has let the budget-anxiety go quiet enough to actually ask the protagonist what he wants,
meaning it, rather than needing the answer for a report.

## Yohei (洋平) — general store

**Age/work**: early 60s. Runs 洋平商店, a general/convenience store that's closer to a "sells
whatever the street still needs" operation — hardware bits, produce, dry goods, cigarettes,
odds and ends. Solid, still-working build; moves his own stock.

**Money**: the shop turns a small, steady profit. Not thriving. Enough.

**Family**: widower — wife passed eleven years ago, mentioned once in passing, never dwelt on.
One adult child, a son, who works in the city in something unrelated (logistics, doesn't matter
which) and has made it clear, gently but clearly, that he isn't coming back to run the store.
Yohei has not brought this up with the son directly in over a year. The store has no clear
successor and Yohei has stopped actively looking for one, which is itself the problem.

**Currently struggling with**: the succession question, which he avoids rather than solves — see
flaw below. Also, practically: the festival stock order (a real, ordinary logistics problem —
extra inventory for the shopping street's seasonal festival, arriving in stages across the month)
gives him something concrete and present-tense to focus on instead.

**How he first treats the protagonist**: brisk, transactional, not unfriendly — sizes him up as
another new face who'll probably be gone in a month, same as most. Warms slowly through repeated,
ordinary contact, not through any single big moment.

**Flaw, specific**: kind but avoidant of anything that requires sitting in discomfort. Will lend
a tool, tell a customer where to find something, cover for Jin without being asked — and will
change the subject, almost reflexively, the instant a conversation turns toward his son, his
wife, or the store's future. Not cold — just unwilling to be the one who opens that door. This
means: if the protagonist wants to actually reach him on the succession question, HE has to be
the one who doesn't let Yohei change the subject, at least once. That's a real choice point, not
a scripted inevitability.

**When the protagonist isn't there**: runs the store, deals with the festival order, sees Jin
(they have known each other over 30 years — mostly comfortable silence, occasional dry ribbing),
sees Miyoko as a supplier/customer relationship (he moves her some produce, she keeps him in
coffee), handles the ordinary friction of running a small shop (a supplier is late, a regular
customer complains about a price).

**30-day change**: doesn't resolve the succession question by simply deciding — he starts, for
the first time in over a year, allowing it to be discussed at all. Whether it resolves toward
the protagonist (事業承継 route), toward finally calling his son, or toward accepting the shop
may just end when he does, depends on what actually happens across the 30 days, not a switch
flipped on Day 30.

## Miyoko (美代子) — 喫茶のどか

**Age/work**: late 60s. Runs 喫茶のどか, a small café, alone, most days. Warm, a little tired —
visibly, some days, not performed.

**Money**: the café barely covers itself; some weeks it doesn't, and she covers the gap from
savings without telling anyone. This is not dramatized as a crisis — it's a quiet, ongoing fact
of her life, and if the protagonist starts genuinely helping out (Day 3's "short-handed" beat and
its recurrences), it is one of the few threads that can plausibly become CAFE_FOOD_SERVICE as a
real, felt-necessary outcome rather than a menu pick.

**Family**: widow — her husband ran the café with her for over twenty years before he died,
roughly six years ago. One daughter, lives two hours away, calls every week or two, gently and
repeatedly suggests Miyoko sell the café and move closer to her and the grandchildren. Miyoko
always says she'll think about it. She has been saying that for at least two years.

**Currently struggling with**: the daughter's suggestion, which she doesn't want to take
seriously and can't quite dismiss either, and the ordinary physical toll of running a café alone
in her late 60s (a bad knee, long days on her feet).

**How she first treats the protagonist**: immediately warm — genuinely, not performed — the café
is her whole social world and a new face is a small event. Talks a little too freely, a little
too fast, about other people in town.

**Flaw, specific**: generous but an unreliable narrator of other people's business — she repeats
things she's overheard across the counter, usually well-meant, sometimes wrong in a specific,
small way (who said what, in what order, to whom). This should produce at least one real,
awkward beat where something she tells the protagonist about Kamiya, or Yohei, or the town,
turns out to be slightly off, and the protagonist has to sort out what actually happened —
without her ever being framed as malicious or foolish for it. It's just how someone who has run
a counter for forty years processes a town's worth of overheard half-conversations.

**When the protagonist isn't there**: runs the café (often at a loss on quiet days), talks to
whoever comes in, deals with her knee, fields her daughter's calls, buys produce from Yohei,
occasionally has Jin in to fix something (a chair, a door).

**30-day change**: the daughter's pressure doesn't vanish. What can genuinely shift, if the
protagonist's involvement is real and repeated (not a single grand gesture), is Miyoko starting
to imagine a version of staying that doesn't mean doing it entirely alone — which is a real,
adult, unglamorous version of a "町に残る" thread that doesn't require it to be about romance.

## Soma Jin (相馬迅) — independent handyman

**Age/work**: 57 — same age as the protagonist, a detail the game should let land once, not
explain. Freelance repair/odd-jobs generalist, no fixed employer, no fixed hours; goes where the
work is. **Frozen canon behavioral rule (unchanged): a tendency to offer one small, legal,
practical suggestion when he sees someone stuck in a repeating or dead-ended situation — not
because he's omniscient or a therapist, just a habit of someone who fixes things for a living.
This trait is frozen and must not be given added narrative weight.**

**Money**: inconsistent — some weeks are full, some are thin. He is not visibly anxious about
this; it's simply the shape of the work he chose, and it is the actual, lived texture of what
"便利屋独立" means as an outcome, not a romanticized one.

**Family**: no scenes with family. Not tragic, not explained — simply not part of his current
life. He lives alone, simply, and that is presented as an ordinary fact, not a wound.

**Currently struggling with**: nothing dramatic — the ordinary churn of freelance work (a slow
week, a job that ran long, a tool that needs replacing he'd rather not pay for yet). His actual
tension is closer to the protagonist's own, unstated: whether to keep doing this alone
indefinitely, or whether something like a small, official handyman/repair operation (with, say,
a second set of hands) would actually suit him better than he's ever let himself consider. He has
never framed it that way out loud.

**How he first treats the protagonist**: neutral to terse. Doesn't perform friendliness. Will
hand over a tool with a two-word explanation and expect the protagonist to figure out the rest
from watching — this is not coldness, it's his actual communication style (see flaw), and it
should read as mildly disorienting to a protagonist used to people explaining their reasoning.

**Flaw, specific**: good with his hands, bad at explaining anything — assumes competence transfers
by watching, gets mildly impatient when it doesn't, rarely notices he hasn't actually told the
protagonist what's expected. Also: does things for people without asking first — fixes something,
mentions it after the fact rather than before — generous, but occasionally steps on someone's own
wish to have done it themselves. This should produce at least one small friction beat with the
protagonist specifically (e.g., fixing something in the trial house before the protagonist gets
the chance to try it himself).

**When the protagonist isn't there**: works whatever job is in front of him — Yohei's stockroom
door, Miyoko's chairs, a leaking roof at the community hall, whatever the town actually needs
that week. Sees Yohei regularly (old friends). Crosses paths with Miyoko and Kamiya at a
professional remove.

**30-day change**: doesn't become a different person. What can genuinely shift is whether he
lets someone work alongside him consistently enough, and well enough, that the idea of not doing
this entirely alone stops being unthinkable — which is the real substance behind
REPAIR_BUSINESS_INDEPENDENT / HANDYMAN_INDEPENDENT as outcomes: not "the protagonist learns
plumbing," but "Jin lets someone in."

## Maki (真希) — new character, introduced ~Day 12-15

The one new named character this Run adds — deliberately just one, tied directly into an
already-established thread (the empty storefront on the shopping street) rather than invented
from nothing.

**Why she exists**: the romance/marriage system needs a viable, ungimmicky candidate who can
support a gradual, multi-day arc with real friction, not a drop-in love interest. Grafting
romance onto Yohei, Miyoko, or Jin would be either age-implausible or would compromise their
existing, non-romantic function in the story. A new character, arriving mid-story with her own
stake in the town, is the honest way to do this.

**Backstory**: mid-50s. Ran a small floral/event-design business in the city for two decades;
burned out badly the year before the game starts, closed it. Her aunt — her mother's sister, who
ran a shop on Challenge Town's shopping street decades ago before closing it (this is, in-world,
the same storefront that has sat empty and been the subject of the recurring "空き店舗"
noticing-thread since Day 1) — died the previous winter and left Maki the property. Maki came to
town initially just to decide whether to sell it. Somewhere in the weeks before the protagonist
arrives, she decided to open a flower shop there instead — a decision the town, including Yohei
and Miyoko, has been watching unfold in fragments (the "何か始めます" notice, the flower crates,
the "まもなく開店" sign) long before she and the protagonist ever speak.

**Personality/flaw**: direct, fast-moving, decisive to a fault — the opposite of the
protagonist's deliberative caution. Good at starting things, has a specific history of walking
away from things once the excitement wears off (this is, in part, why her city business burned
out — not lack of skill, lack of follow-through once the initial momentum faded). She would
frame this as being honest with herself about what isn't working; it can also look, to someone
watching, like flightiness. This is the real source of value-clash with the protagonist (see
Romance below) — not a manufactured misunderstanding, an actual difference in how they're wired.

**Family**: no living immediate family beyond the inherited property; a brief, distant reference
to a marriage in her 30s that ended without children. Not dwelt on.

**30-day arc**: arrives with the shop as a fresh start, meets the protagonist through ordinary
contact (he's around the shopping street; Jin ends up doing repair work for her shop, which is
the natural bridge — reuse the existing "flower shop needs help" world-thread as the actual
introduction scene, not a coincidence). What develops between them, if the player pursues it, has
to survive at least one real clash over the pace/permanence question (she moves fast, he moves
slow; she's is unsettled by how careful he is, he's unsettled by how easily she'd leave if this
place stopped working for her) before either of them decides anything. See Romance/Marriage,
below, for the condition structure.

## 15 Career Destinations — discovery routes (≥3 each)

Menu-of-jobs is explicitly banned. These are never shown to the player as a list; they exist so
that specific scenes can be authored (Screenplay) and specific state signals can accumulate
(implementation, later, out of scope this Run) toward a destination the player never picked from
a menu.

1. **工場・製造 (factory/manufacturing)** — (a) Kamiya's listings board, browsed idly, one line
   catches the eye without being pursued yet; (b) a chance conversation at the community hall with
   someone who works the floor at the town's remaining small factory; (c) Yohei mentions a former
   customer who took a factory job after his own trial residency, as an aside, not a pitch.
2. **トラック運転手 (truck driver)** — (a) Kamiya's listings; (b) watching delivery trucks on the
   shopping street at the exact hour Yohei's stock comes in, a driver they get to know by sight;
   (c) Jin mentions a driving job he turned down because he prefers not having a fixed route.
3. **配送・物流 (delivery/logistics)** — (a) helping Yohei count and shelve a stock delivery;
   (b) Kamiya's listings; (c) noticing how much of the shopping street's daily rhythm is actually
   about things arriving and being moved, articulated as an observation, not a lecture.
4. **修繕・設備 (facility repair)** — (a) borrowing Yohei's toolbox, fixing the trial house's own
   shelf; (b) Jin's half-day shadow invitation; (c) the community hall's leaking roof, a job Jin
   does on-screen whether or not the protagonist is there to see it, that can be joined later.
5. **商店・小売 (shop/retail)** — (a) helping Yohei count inventory; (b) covering the register for
   ten minutes while Yohei steps out; (c) Kamiya mentioning a vacant retail slot elsewhere in town,
   unconnected to Yohei's, as a genuinely separate option (so retail doesn't collapse into "become
   Yohei" by default).
6. **飲食 (food service)** — (a) helping Miyoko on a short-handed day; (b) tasting a menu
   experiment; (c) noticing, unprompted, how much of the café's real work is the between-orders
   downtime, not the coffee itself.
7. **介護・生活支援 (caregiving/life support)** — (a) Kamiya's half-day facility experience offer;
   (b) noticing Miyoko's knee and the physical toll of her days, without her asking for help;
   (c) an elderly community-hall regular who needs something small carried or fixed, unconnected to
   Jin's work specifically.
8. **AI開発者 (AI developer)** — (a) a free-text conversation where the protagonist volunteers
   real interest in computers/software, which Kamiya files away; (b) helping Yohei with the
   festival-order paperwork by hand, then wondering aloud if there's a better way; (c) overhearing
   Kamiya on the phone, frustrated, about the Center's own outdated case-tracking spreadsheet.
9. **中小企業AI/DX支援 (SME digital support)** — (a) fixing/streamlining Yohei's paperwork in a
   way that visibly saves him real time, more than once; (b) Kamiya connecting the protagonist to
   a local business owner (not Yohei) who has a similar, separate problem; (c) Maki's flower shop
   bookkeeping, a natural late-arc route once she's introduced.
10. **便利屋独立 (independent handyman)** — (a) the half-day shadow job with Jin; (b) being handed
    tools with no explanation and having to learn by doing, repeatedly, across several days;
    (c) Jin mentioning, once, almost accidentally, that a second set of hands he trusted would
    actually change how much work he could take.
11. **修繕業独立 (independent repair business)** — (a) repeated shadow days with Jin, not just
    one; (b) the community hall roof job, if joined; (c) a conversation where Jin — uncharacteristically
    — asks what the protagonist actually wants, rather than just handing over a tool.
12. **小規模店舗開業 (opening a small shop)** — (a) the empty-storefront thread, followed across
    many days before Maki is even introduced; (b) Maki's shop opening, seen from outside first;
    (c) a direct conversation with Maki about what opening a shop from nothing actually took.
13. **地元事業承継 (local business succession)** — (a) Yohei's unguarded "この店、継ぐ奴がいない
    んだよな"; (b) a repeated pattern of practical help at the shop that starts to look, to Yohei
    and to the player, like more than a customer's kindness; (c) a direct, protagonist-initiated
    conversation that doesn't let Yohei change the subject (a real choice point, not automatic).
14. **アルバイト等で保留 (part-time / undecided)** — (a) explicitly choosing not to pursue any
    thread with focus, several days running; (b) telling Kamiya directly, in a free-text beat,
    that he isn't ready to decide; (c) simply running out of days without ever committing — this
    route requires no special scene, only the absence of the others, which is itself the point.
15. **無職 (unemployed)** — same shape as 14 but further: actively declining offers (Kamiya's
    connections, Jin's implicit opening, Yohei's shop) when they come, not merely staying busy
    elsewhere. Not written as a punishment ending — see Ending.

Additional discovery texture that applies across several careers at once: Kamiya's DAY5 and
DAY15 check-ins are the only points where accumulated signals are ever named back to the player,
and even then only as an observation ("最近、この辺によく顔を出してるみたいですね"), never as a
menu of options to pick from.

## Life-status axis (separate from career)

町に残る / 引っ越す / 独身 / 恋愛関係 / 結婚 / 婿入り — tracked independently of career. A player
can end 無職 and 町に残る, or AI開発者 and 引っ越す (the work Kamiya connects him to may not be
in Challenge Town at all), or 地元事業承継 and 婿入り if the Yohei and Maki threads both land in
a specific combination worth naming here explicitly:

**婿入り condition (地元事業承継 + 婿入り combined ending)**: this specific combination requires
BOTH the Yohei succession thread reaching genuine trust (route 13, all three sub-beats
essentially in play) AND the Maki romance thread reaching its own late-game commitment point —
and critically, these two threads are not naturally the same thread (Yohei's shop and Maki's
shop are different businesses). The honest version of "婿入り" in this story is therefore tied to
Maki specifically (marrying into the property she inherited, per her backstory above), not to
Yohei. A player pursuing both Yohei's succession AND Maki's romance ends up owning/running two
separate connections to the town — which is a legitimate, richly-earned "町に残る" ending on its
own, without needing to force them into one business. This should be stated plainly in the
Ending section so implementation doesn't later invent a contrived merger.

## Romance / Marriage — required conditions

Never triggered by a single scene or a single good choice. Minimum shape, all required:
1. Multiple days of ordinary contact (not save-the-date events — just repeated presence).
2. At least one shared task/experience with real stakes for her, not staged for the player (e.g.
   helping with the shop opening itself).
3. A genuine value alignment moment — something the protagonist does or says that she couldn't
   have gotten from anyone else in town, specifically because of who he is (his caution, his
   competence, his particular grief-shaped attentiveness).
4. A genuine value clash — the pace/permanence conflict described above, played as a real
   disagreement, not a misunderstanding solved by one apology.
5. Distance — at least one stretch of days where the thread goes quiet, by circumstance, not by
   player inaction; the relationship has to survive not being tended constantly.
6. An actual choice, late (Day 25+), where staying with her/the town costs something concretely
   named (see Day 26-29).

Marriage itself is not resolved inside 30 days — see Ending. What resolves is whether the
relationship is heading there, visibly, by Day 30.

## Structural principles

**Day 15 (midpoint)**: not a lecture, not a stats screen. The protagonist should encounter,
as an ordinary event, the gap between the plan he had on Day 1 ("decide the next job within a
month, methodically, the way he'd have run a project") and whatever has actually been
accumulating — half-formed, contradictory, more about people than about a decision matrix. The
event should be small and specific: e.g. he goes to write something down — a list, a comparison,
the kind of document his old job trained him to make — and finds he can't make the columns work,
because half of what's pulling at him isn't comparable on a spreadsheet. No one tells him this.

**Day 20-25**: real constraints enter, not abstractly. An actual hiring window closing at
whichever business Kamiya connected him to. Actual money — the shape of what 30 more days without
income would cost him, concretely, once. An actual scheduling collision — two things he cares
about landing on the same day, forcing an explicit choice, not a soft "you can always do both
later." Not everything can stay open anymore.

**Day 26-29**: choosing starts costing something specific, named on screen, not abstractly
implied. If he commits toward Yohei's shop, he is explicitly not free to also take the out-of-town
opening Kamiya found. If he commits toward Maki, the "just decide about a job, alone, cleanly" plan
he arrived with is definitively not the shape his life is taking. The game does not punish him for
this — it simply stops pretending everything is still available.

**Day 30**: begins as an ordinary morning, not a menu. The people, places, promises, and failures
of the past 29 days come back on their own — Yohei is doing something ordinary and present, not
staged as a farewell tour; Miyoko's café is open as always; Jin is or isn't around depending on
whether there's work; Kamiya, if a meeting was ever scheduled, keeps it. The decision is made at
the end of an ordinary day that happens to be day 30, not inside a ceremony built for the
occasion.

## Ending — no scoring

No BEST/BAD END. The ending is a specific combination: which career signal (if any) actually
became a commitment, which life-status the protagonist chose, which relationships are still
present versus which quietly lapsed from neglect, what was explicitly given up to get there.
無職 is a valid, non-punished ending — the game should be able to write it with the same care as
any other, including one small scene of what a next, undecided day looks like, not a fade to
black.

## Conflict inventory (present somewhere in DAY1-30, not all in DAY1-7)

Misunderstanding (Miyoko's imperfect gossip; Kamiya's early pattern-matching of the protagonist).
Failure (a repair attempt that doesn't take; a pitch to a local business that goes nowhere).
Schedule collision (two things on one day, Day 20-25 range). Value clash (protagonist vs. Maki's
pace, at minimum). Being turned down (a job that goes to someone else because he waited — see
Player Absence). Kindness landing wrong (Jin fixing something before the protagonist could try).
The protagonist not being right (his assessment of how Yohei's shop "should" be run is at least
partly wrong, and someone tells him so).

## Player absence — concrete mechanics for the story (not implementation)

At minimum: (a) if the protagonist never engages Miyoko's short-handed thread, she manages
anyway, visibly more tired, and a later scene should let the player notice the cost without being
told to feel guilty about it; (b) at least one real job opportunity Kamiya surfaces has a
deadline that can pass unengaged, and does — filled by someone else, mentioned in passing, not as
a punishment beat, just as true; (c) Maki's shop opens on its own calendar regardless of whether
the protagonist is present for any specific step of it.
